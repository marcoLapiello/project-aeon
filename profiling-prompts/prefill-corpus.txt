The history of cartography is the study of how maps, from the earliest known
sketches on clay tablets to the digital atlases of the present day, have been
made, used, and understood. Maps are not neutral records of the world. Every map
selects, simplifies, and arranges, and each of those choices carries assumptions
about what matters. A map of a coastline drawn for sailors will show depths and
harbours; the same coast drawn for an engineer will show elevation and the paths
of rivers, and the two may share almost no features at all. For this reason
historians read maps as arguments rather than as pictures, asking who made them,
for whom, and what they wished their readers to see.

The earliest surviving maps are modest. Babylonian clay tablets from roughly the
sixth century before the common era show a flat disc of land ringed by a river
and set within an outer ocean, and they place the city of Babylon at the centre.
Whether such a drawing was meant to be navigated or merely contemplated is not
knowable from the object alone, and the question is typical of the field. Later
Greek writers approached the problem differently. Eratosthenes, working in
Alexandria in the third century before the common era, reasoned from the length
of shadows at two places a known distance apart and produced a value for the
circumference of the earth that is within a few percent of the modern figure.
His method was geometric and his result was quantitative, and it marked a shift
from the symbolic to the measurable that his successors inherited.

Roman practice turned the Greek inheritance toward administration. The surviving
fragments of Roman road maps suggest long, narrow scrolls, impractical to hold
in the hand but convenient to unroll along the length of a route, and inscribed
with the distances between stopping places. These were not representations of
space so much as instruments of travel. When the empire's road network decayed,
so did the demand for such instruments, and for several centuries in Europe the
production of maps narrowed to the schematic diagrams that monasteries drew to
accompany descriptions of the holy places, in which geography served the needs
of pilgrimage and the figures of particular cities might be drawn out of
proportion to any measurable distance between them.

The Chinese tradition developed along its own lines and, in several respects,
ahead of the European one. The surviving examples of Chinese cartography show
sustained attention to the systematic grid, to measured distance, and to the
careful delineation of rivers and coastlines. The invention of printing gave the
later Chinese maps an advantage that manuscript maps could not match, since a
single woodblock could yield hundreds of identical copies, each carrying the
same information to a different reader. That advantage was not simply one of
volume. A map that can be copied exactly becomes an object that can be corrected
and compared, and the practice of comparison is the beginning of a shared
standard.

In Europe the recovery of Ptolemy's geography in the fifteenth century did more
than supply a set of coordinates. It supplied a method, and, perhaps more
important, it supplied an authority whose errors were difficult to correct
because they were embedded in a system. Sailors who had crossed the Atlantic
found Ptolemy's figures wanting, and the slow, contentious process of replacing
them with observation is one of the central stories of the age of exploration.
The instruments that made the replacement possible, the astrolabe, the quadrant,
and eventually the marine chronometer, were in many cases as expensive to
develop as the ships they guided, and their adoption was uneven and contested.

Surveying, meanwhile, became a profession. The great national surveys of the
eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, undertaken for military and fiscal reasons
and later for the sheer ambition of the thing, produced the first accurate maps
of entire countries. That work demanded triangulation on a scale no private
individual could fund, and it established a durable alliance between the map and
the state. From that alliance came the conventions that later cartographers
treated as natural: the north at the top, a uniform scale, a measured grid, and
the systematic suppression of detail judged irrelevant to the purpose at hand.

The twentieth century added two pressures. The first was aerial photography,
which turned the surface of the earth into something that could be recorded
rather than only drawn, and which forced a distinction between the base image and
the map compiled from it. The second was the computer, which made it possible to
store a description of the world in a form that could be queried, combined, and
rendered at any scale. The digital map does not resolve the old questions about
selection and arrangement. It conceals them, since a rendering at the touch of a
button can hide the fact that someone, somewhere, decided which features would be
recorded and which would be omitted from the data that everyone now shares.

What has remained constant across all of these changes is the map's position
between knowledge and action. A map is bound to a decision, whether it is the
decision of a captain to take one channel rather than another, a minister to tax
one district more heavily than its neighbour, or a traveller to walk a path that
seems shorter on the page. It is never a complete account of a place, and it
would be of little use if it were, since completeness and legibility are usually
opposed. The historian's task is to recover the purposes that shaped a map, and
in doing so to recover, as far as the surviving evidence allows, something of the
worldview of the people who made and used it. That task grows harder as the
instruments change, because the network of decisions behind a digital map is
distributed across many hands and is rarely written down. It has not, however,
become less necessary.
